 Synthesis 


          On August 26, 2026, a group observation was conducted to analyze the interactions between KoolKids sellers and their customers during lunchtime from 12:10 to 1:00 PM. Two customers were observed at different times, with Joshua Lorenzo Chua and Hannah Sofia Roque carefully taking notes of all exchanges to serve as the primary data for the study. The language used was noted, including Filipino, English, and Taglish, depending on the situation, with the use of "po" to show a respectful attitude. Daphne Carreon captured photos, while Cecil Erikah Paringit filmed the interactions to provide supplementary material for translation and review. These recordings supported the group’s notes, ensuring accurate information on how they communicate in an interactive model of communication.

          After starting the first observation period, the first customer was Lyle Joy Ortiz, a student at PSHS-CLC in CFZ. Ortiz began the conversation by asking, “Uhh, chocolate po ba ‘to?” The seller confirmed, “Oo, chocolate,” to which Ortiz responded, “Ahhh, sige po ‘yun na lang.” The seller then asked, “Ilan iyo? and Ortiz answered, “Isa lang po.” The transaction concluded with the seller saying, “79 lang. Sukli mo, Lyle,” while extending his hand. This exchange demonstrated casual syntax, polite semantics, and used fillers like “uhh” and “oh,” along with nonverbal cues such as nodding and gestures.

          The second observation in the same observation period was Ma’am Pauline Kirstie Atumpag, a social science teacher at PSHS-CLC in CFZ. The seller greeted her politely, saying, “Good afternoon po. Anong inyo?” Atumpag ordered, “Isang coffee jelly and try ko ‘yung ube special.” The seller confirmed the payment, stating, “198 po lahat. I received 200 po.” Atumpag then asked, “Saan ako maghihintay?” and the seller responded, “Kahit saan po, puwede niyo na lang balikan.” Throughout the interaction, a respectful attitude, eye contact, and clear communication were evident, although noise from the blender and nearby conversations occasionally interfered.

          In the second session on September 9, 2026, customers Joseph Dos Mongcal and Matteo Redillas interacted with the seller. Mongcal began by saying, “Uhhh, isang blueberry po, ay, dalawang blueberry po pala.” The seller asked, “Tapos? Kasama ka ba niya?” Redillas then replied, “Tapos, isa pong choco mousse jelly.” The seller clarified, “Magkasama na ba kayo ng bayad?” After a nod from the customers, the seller requested a name. Mongcal responded, “Under Angelo po.” Payment was handed over, and the seller confirmed, “Thank you.”

          During the claiming of orders, the seller asked, “Sino kasi umorder ng choco mousse?” Redillas raised his hand and received his drink. For Mongcal, the seller offered extra: “Medyo naparami gawa ko, gusto mo bigyan kita ng extra?” Mongcal laughed and agreed, later experiencing a brain freeze and reacting physically by moving around to ease the cold sensation. This exchange highlighted nonverbal cues such as nodding, raising hands, and gestures, alongside vocalics like fillers such as "uhhh” and laughter. Background noise from blenders, gadgets, and chatter was present, requiring adjustments in speaking volume.

          Overall, the communication patterns in both observations were similar, being casual yet respectful and shaped by verbal cues such as syntax, semantics, pragmatics, and phonology, alongside nonverbal cues including kinesics, oculesics, chromatics, vocalics, and olfactics. Environmental noise created barriers, but sellers and customers adapted through repetition, gestures, and volume adjustments. Occasional confusion over shared names emphasized the need for attentiveness. The colors of the drinks on the menu functioned as chromatics, visually distinguishing products and drawing attention, while the pleasant aroma of freshly prepared drinks served as olfactics, attracting nearby students and encouraging engagement. Eye contact between buyers and sellers reinforced oculesics, while vocal fillers such as “uhhh,” “ahhh,” “hmm,” and “huh,” along with understandable speaking speed, varied volume, and moderate pitch, highlighted vocalics. Although the transactions were not completely smooth, sellers managed them effectively, demonstrating adaptability and clear communication despite challenges.




 Analysis 


          During the group process observation of the KoolKids sellers and their customers, it can be seen that they follow an interactive model. Both sellers and buyers are senders and receivers but unlike the transactional model, they do not speak simultaneously but wait for their turn. The communication process mainly revolves around the ordering and purchasing of drinks, with the sellers demonstrating their field of experience in managing the store and communicating with customers, while the customers’ field of experience was in purchasing and ordering items. Filipino, Taglish, and English were used depending on the situation, and the communication was generally casual and conversational since the situation was simple transactions, while the use of “po” reflected a respectful attitude.

          Both verbal and nonverbal cues were observed throughout the interaction. The verbal cues included syntax, semantics, pragmatics, and phonology, while the nonverbal cues included oculesics, kinesics, chromatics, vocalics, and olfactics.

          For the verbal cues, syntax was observed through the use of casual and sometimes grammatically incomplete expressions. The sellers and customers did not seem concerned with grammatical correctness, as the main purpose of the communication was the quick, clear, and complete transaction. Semantics was evident in the meanings conveyed by phrases such as “Sa ‘yo po ba ‘to?”, “Kay ano po,” and “Magkano po?”, which were mainly used to identify orders, confirm customers, and ask about prices. Pragmatics was shown through the use of Filipino, Taglish, and English, as well as polite expressions such as “po,” which were appropriate for the casual yet respectful interaction. Phonology was also evident through fillers such as “um,” “ah,” “hmm,” and “huh,”

          For the nonverbal cues, oculesics was observed through the sellers’ and the customers’ eye contact and visual attention toward one another and the menu. Kinesics included pointing at the menu, nodding or shaking their heads, raising a hand when being called or asking for attention, and pointing toward one's head to communicate a reaction to the cold drink. These gestures helped clarify and support the verbal messages exchanged. Chromatics may also be observed through the colors of the drinks displayed on the menu, which helped visually distinguish the different products. Vocalics was seen through changes in volume, pitch, and speaking speed depending on the surrounding noise. The speakers generally maintained an understandable pace and moderate pitch. Olfactics was present through the smell of the drinks being prepared, which appeared to attract the attention of nearby students.

          Several sources of physical noise were observed during the interaction, including the blender, rain, phones and other gadgets playing at high volumes, and students talking loudly near the area. The loud conversations of students also served as a physical barrier, as they sometimes made it difficult for the sellers and customers to hear one another clearly. This required the communicators to adjust their speaking volume or pay closer attention to the messages being exchanged. There was also a possibility of confusion when customers or sellers had the same name, which could affect the identification of the intended receiver.
